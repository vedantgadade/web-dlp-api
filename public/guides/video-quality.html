Video quality guide

Understanding 144p, 720p, 1080p, 1440p and 4K.

A quality number describes approximate vertical resolution. Resolution is important, but it is not the only factor that determines how a video looks or how large its file will be.

Common options

  • 144p–480p: lower-resolution choices that can reduce file size and data use.
  • 720p: commonly called HD and often a practical choice for smaller screens.
  • 1080p: commonly called Full HD and widely used for phones, laptops, and TVs.
  • 1440p: commonly called QHD and provides more pixels than 1080p.
  • 2160p: commonly called 4K UHD and normally requires substantially more storage and bandwidth.

Resolution is not the same as quality

A 1080p label tells you the approximate resolution, not the complete visual quality. Bitrate, compression, frame rate, the original recording, and the source encoding also matter. A well-encoded 720p video can sometimes look better than a poorly encoded 1080p video.

Why is 4K not always available?

A downloader cannot create genuine source detail that does not exist. VGSAVE shows standard options based on the source media and does not promise a quality that the source cannot provide. If the source only offers 720p, selecting 4K would not produce genuine 4K detail.

Higher quality means a larger file

Higher resolution often requires more data. A 4K file can therefore take longer to prepare, use more storage, and take longer to download than a lower-resolution version. If you are saving a video for a phone, choosing the highest number is not always necessary.

Which option should you choose?

Choose the highest available quality that makes sense for your screen, storage, and intended use. For quick mobile viewing, a lower option may be enough. For a large TV or editing workflow, a higher-quality source may be more useful when it is genuinely available.
